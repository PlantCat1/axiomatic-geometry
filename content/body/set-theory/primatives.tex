\chapter{Primitive Notions}
For our axiomatic system, we adopt three primitive notions, consisting of one object and two relations, which together form a single structure. The first of these is the point, denoted by upper case latin letters, $A, B, C, \dots$. Each point is a member of the point-set $\mathbf{P}$, which is defined as $\mathbf{P} \coloneqq \{P \mid P \text{ is a point} \}$. This set is equipped with a ternary relation $\mathcal{B}$ (betweenness) and an quaternary relation $\cong$(congruence). Together, the domain and these two relations constitute the plane $\mathcal{P}$:
\[ \mathcal{P} \coloneqq \langle \mathbf{P}, \mathcal{B}, \cong \rangle.\]

\section{Plane}

The plane is the structure consisting of the domain $\mathbf{P}$ together with the relations $\mathcal{B}$ and $\cong$.

\begin{definition}[Plane]A plane is a structure
    \[
    \mathcal{P} \coloneqq \langle \mathbf{P}, \mathcal{B}, \cong \rangle.
    \]
    Where $\mathbf{P}$ is the domain.
\end{definition}

\section{Points}
The elements of the domain $\mathbf{P}$ are termed \textit{points} and denoted using capital italics such as $A$, $B$, $C$, $\dots$. At this foundational level, a point is defined not via decomposition into simpler parts, but solely by its membership in this set.
characterization
Intuitively, a point can be understood as a unique position on the plane. This corresponds to the Euclidean characterization of a point as "that which has no part". A point can be informally regarded as having no magnitude, length, width, or depth, and as being zero-dimensional. However, within the formal system, this physical characterization is set aside and a point is treated strictly as a primitive object with no further definition, thus, the characterization of these objects carries no weight in the formal deduction.

\section{Betweenness}

Betweenness is a ternary relation on the set of all points, denoted by $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathbf{P}^3$. Given points $A$, $B$, and $C$, we write $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$ and read it as "$B$ lies between $A$ and $C$". 

Unlike a set $\{A, B, C\}$, where the order of elements is immaterial, betweenness is defined over the cartesian product $\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{P}$. Thus it depends on the order of the arguments. Formally,
\[
\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathbf{P}^3 \coloneqq \{(A, B, C) \mid A, B, C \in \mathbf{P}\}.
\]
The expression $\mathcal{B}(A, B, C)$ is simply the assertion that $(A, B, C) \in \mathcal{B}$.

In Euclidean geometry a point $B$ is between $A$ and $C$ precisely when the three points lie on a line and $B$ is met while traveling from $A$ to $C$ along that line.Our axioms capture this idea without invoking lines or order directly.

We now x five axioms governing the behavior of $\mathcal{B}$.

\subsection{Axioms of Betweenness}

\begin{axiom}[Symmetry of Betweenness]
    \[
    \forall A, B, C \in \mathbf{P}, \mathcal{B}(A, B, C) \implies \mathcal{B}(C, B, A).
    \]
\end{axiom}
Betweenness is symmetric with respect to the outer points: if $B$ lies between $A$ and $C$, then it also lies between $C$ and $A$.  This reflects the fact that the segment $AC$ is the same as the segment $CA$. Left-to-right implication is used over a biconditional due to redundancy, as the right-to-left implication directly follows from this form of the axiom.

\begin{axiom}[Order]
  $\forall A,B,X\in\mathbf{P},\ \mathcal{B}(A,B,X)\ \implies\
  \bigl(\,\neg\mathcal{B}(B,A,X)\ \land\ \neg\mathcal{B}(A,X,B)\,\bigr).$
\end{axiom}
If $B$ is between $A$ and $X$, then $A$ cannot be between $B$ and $X$, nor can $X$ be between $A$ and $B$. In other words, a point can serve as a ``middle'' in only one way: the triple $(A,B,X)$ determines a unique order along the line. Together with the Identity axiom, this implies that $\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$ is impossible (otherwise, taking $A=B=X$, we would have both $\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$ and its negation). Thus the relation is \emph{non-reflexive} in the strict sense: no point lies between itself and itself.

\begin{axiom}[Identity of Betweenness]
    \[
    \forall A, B \in \mathbf{P}, \mathcal{B}(A, B, A) \implies A = B.
    \]
\end{axiom}
This axiom says that if a point appears as the "middle" of a triple that begins and ends with the same point, then the two outer points must coincide. In particular, it forbids the possibility that a distinct point $B$ $B$ lies between $A$ and $A$; such a configuration would contradict the intuition that betweenness requires a genuine interval. Notice that the converse, $A=B\implies\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$, is \emph{not} assumed; indeed the next axiom will exclude it.

\begin{axiom}[Density]
  $\forall A,C \in \mathbf{P}, A \neq C \implies
  \exists B \in \mathbf{P} \colon \mathcal{B}(A,B,C).$
\end{axiom}
Whenever two points are distinct, there exists a point strictly between
them.  This captures the idea that segments are dense—between any two
points one can always find a third.

\begin{axiom}[Extensibility]
  \[
  \forall A,B\in\mathbf{P},\ A\neq B\ \implies\
  \exists C\in\mathbf{P}\colon\ \mathcal{B}(A,B,C).
  \]
\end{axiom}
Given two distinct points, there is always a point beyond the second,
so that the second lies between the first and the new point.  This
guarantees that lines extend indefinitely.

\begin{axiom}[Transitivity of Betweenness]
    $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C) \land \mathcal{B}(B,C,D) \land B \neq C \implies \mathcal{B}(A,B,D)$.
\end{axiom}

If $B$ lies between $A$ and $C$, and $C$ lies between $B$ and $D$ (with $B\neq C$), then $B$ lies between $A$ and $D$. This axiom allows us to chain betweenness relations along a line.

These six axioms are mutually independent: no one can be derived from the others (as can be shown by standard model-theoretic arguments). Together with the congruence axioms to be introduced later, they will form the basis of our geometric theory.

\begin{axiom}
    $\exists A,B \in \mathbf{P}, A \neq B.$
\end{axiom}

\begin{theorem}[Irreflexivity of Betweenness]
    No point lies between it and itself:
    \[
    \forall A \in \mathbf{P},\; \neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,A).
    \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Assume $\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$. By the order axiom, this implies $\neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$ and $\neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$, which contradicts our assumption. Hence, $\neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Distinct Endpoints]
    Betweenness forces distinct outer points:
    \[
    \mathcal{B}(A,B,C) \implies A \neq C.
    \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Suppose $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$ and $A = C$. Then, we have $\mathcal{B}(A,B,A)$, which by the identity of betweenness implies $A = B$. Substituting back gives $\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$, which contradicts the irreflexivity of betweenness. Therefore $A \neq C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Strict Betweenness]
    If $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$, then $A \neq B$ and $B \neq C$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$ and $A = B$. Then, $\mathcal{B}(A,A,C)$, which implies $\neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,C)$ by the order of betweenness. However, this is a contradiction. Thus, $A \neq B$.
Similarly, if we assume $B = C$, then we have $\mathcal{B}(A,B,B) \implies \neg \mathcal{B}(A,B,B)$, leading to a contridiction. Thus, $B \neq C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Contrapositive Ordering]
    For all $A,B,X \in \mathbf{P}$,
    \[
    \mathcal{B}(B,A,X) \implies \neg\mathcal{B}(A,B,X)
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \mathcal{B}(A,X,B) \implies \neg\mathcal{B}(A,B,X).
    \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    The Order axiom gives the two implications
    \[
    \mathcal{B}(A,B,X) \implies \neg\mathcal{B}(B,A,X)
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \mathcal{B}(A,B,X) \implies \neg\mathcal{B}(A,X,B).
    \]
    Taking the logical contrapositive of each implication(Iwhich is logically equivalent to the original), yields precisely the two stated results.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Symmetry Extended]
    Given $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$ none of the following hold:

    $\mathcal{B}(B,A,C)$, $\mathcal{B}(A,C,B)$, $\mathcal{B}(B,C,A)$, $\mathcal{B}(C,A,B)$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Congruence}
Congruence is a quaternary relation on the set of all points denoted by $\cong \subseteq \mathbf{P}^4$. Rather than mapping individual points to one another, this relation establishes a geometric equivalence between two pairs of points. We write $\cong (A,B,C,D)$, to assert that the segment from point $A$ to point $B$ is congruent to the segment from point $C$ to point $D$. Formally:
\[
\cong (A,B,C,D) \iff (A,B,C,D) \in \cong.
\]

To reflect this conceptually, we bundle the point pairs $(A,B)$ and $(C,D)$ into the typographical units $AB$ and $CD$ and introduce the infix symbol $\cong$:
\[
AB \cong CD \overset{\text{def}}{\iff} \cong(A,B,C,D)
\]
If the relation holds for the pairs $(A, B)$ and $(C, D)$, we write $(A, B) \cong (C, D)$, which is interpreted as the "distance" from $A$ to $B$ being equal to the distance from $C$ to $D$. 

We now impose three axioms governing the behavior of $\cong$.

\begin{axiom}[Reversal of Congruence]
    \[
    \forall A, B \in \mathbf{P},\; AB \cong BA.
    \]
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Reflexivity of Congruence]
    \[
    \forall A, B \in \mathbf{P},\; AB \cong AB.
    \]
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Transitivity of Congruence]
    \[
    \forall A, B, C, D, E, F \in \mathbf{P},\; (AB \cong CD \land AB \cong EF) \implies CD \cong EF.
    \]
\end{axiom}

\begin{theorem}[Symmetry of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$, then $CD \cong AB$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We are given $AB \cong CD$. From the reflexivity of congruence axiom we also have $AB \cong AB$. Applying these two congruences to the transitivity axiom gives the antecedent
    \[
    AB \cong CD \land AB \cong AB,
    \]
    which implies $CD \cong AB$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Left-Reversal of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$, then $BA \cong CD$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We are given $AB \cong CD$. From the reversal of congruence, we also have $AB \cong BA$. Applying transitivity to the conjunction
    \[
    AB \cong BA \land AB \cong CD
    \]
    yields $BA \cong CD$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Right-Reversal of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$, then $AB \cong DC$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We are given $AB \cong CD$. From the reversal of congruence, we have $CD \cong DC$, and by the symmetry of congruence we have $CD \cong AB$. Applying transitivity to
    \[
    CD \cong AB \land CD \cong DC,
    \]
    yields $AB \cong DC$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Full-Reversal of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$, then $BA \cong DC$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    From $AB \cong CD$, left-reversal gives $BA \cong CD$. Applying right-reversal to $BA \cong CD$ yields $BA \cong DC$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Full-Transitivity of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$ and $CD \cong EF$, then $AB \cong EF$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We first apply symmetry to the first premise, giving $CD \cong AB$.  Then, we apply this result along with the second premise to the  transitivity axiom, giving the antecedent
    \[
    CD \cong AB \land CD \cong EF.
    \]
    This implies $AB \cong EF$, thus proving the result.
\end{proof}

With these results, we have demonstrated that $\cong$ is an equivalence relation on ordered pairs of points, as it is reflexive (by axiom) and symmetric (by theorem), and fully transitive (by theorem).

Because $\cong$ is an equivalence relation, it partitions the set of all ordered point pairs into disjoint equivalence classes. Each class consists of all segments that are congruent to one another, which in geometric terms corresponds to the set of all segments having the same length. This formally captures the intuitive idea that congruence of segments is equality of distance.
