\chapter{Primative Notions}
For our axiomatic system, we adopt three primative notions, consisting of one object and two relations, which together form a single structure. The first of these is the point, denoted by upper case latin letters, \(A, B, C, \dots\). Each point is a member of the point-set \(\mathbf{P}\), which is defined as \(\mathbf{P} \coloneq \{P \mid P \text{ is a point} \}\). This set is equipped with a ternary relation \(\mathcal{B}\) (betwenessness) and an quadernary relation \(\cong\) (congruence). Together, the domain and these two relations constitute the plane \(\mathcal{P}\):
\[ \mathcal{P} \coloneq \langle \mathbf{P}, \mathcal{B}, \cong \rangle.\]

\section{Plane}

The plane is the structure consisting of the domain $\mathbf{P}$ together with the relations $\mathcal{B}$ and $\cong$.

\begin{definition}[Plane]
    A plane is a structure
    \[
    \mathcal{P} \coloneqq \langle \mathbf{P}, \mathcal{B}, \cong \rangle.
    \]
    Where $\mathbf{P}$ is the domain.
\end{definition}

\section{Points}
The elements of the domain $\mathbf{P}$ are termed \textit{points} and denoted using capital italics such as $A$, $B$, $C$, $\dots$.. At this foundational level, a point is defined not via decomposition into simplier parts, but solely by its membership in this set.

Intuitvely, a point can be understood as a unique position on the plane. This corresponds to the Euclidean charactarize of a point as "that which has no part". A point can be informally regarded as having no magnitude, length, width, or depth, and as being zero-dimensional. However, within the formal system, this physical charactarization is set aside and a point is treated strictly as a primative object with no further definition, thus, the charactarization of these objects carries no weight in the formal deduction.

\section{Betweenness}

Betweenness is a ternary relation on the set of all points, denoted by $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathbf{P}^3$. Given points $A$, $B$, and $C$, we write $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$ and read it as "$B$ lies between $A$ and $C$". 

Unlike a set $\{A, B, C\}$, where the order of elements is immaterial, the betweeness is defined over the cartesian product $\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{P}$. Thus it depends on the order of the arguments. Formally,
\[
\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathbf{P}^3 \coloneqq \{(A, B, C) \mid A, B, C \in \mathbf{P}\}.
\]
The expression $\mathcal{B}(A, B, C)$ is simply the assertion that $(A, B, C) \in \mathcal{B}$.

To lighten notation we introduce the square-bracket shorthand:
\[
\left[ABC\right] \overset{\text{def}}{\iff} \mathcal{B}(A,B,C).
\]

begin ai:
\noindent\textbf{Intuitive picture.}
In Euclidean geometry a point $B$ is between $A$ and $C$ precisely when
the three points lie on a line and $B$ is met while traveling from
$A$ to $C$ along that line.  Our axioms capture this idea without
invoking lines or order directly.

We now impose five axioms that govern the behaviour of $\mathcal{B}$.

We now impose five axioms governing the behavior of $\mathcal{B}$.
\begin{axiom}[Identity of Betweeness]
    \[
    \forall A, B \in \mathbf{P}, \mathcal{B}(A, B, A) \implies A = B.
    \]
\end{axiom}
This axiom says that if a point appears as the "middle" of a triple that begins and ends with the same point, then the two outer points must coincide. In particular, it forbids the possibity that a distinct point $B$ $B$ lies between $A$ and $A$; such a configuration would contradict the intuition that betweenness requires a genuine interval. Notice that the converse, $A=B\implies\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$, is \emph{not} assumed; indeed the next axiom will exclude it.

\begin{axiom}[Symmetry of Betweenness]
    \[
    \forall A, B, C \in \mathbf{P}, \mathcal{B}(A, B, C) \implies \mathcal{B}(C, B, A).
    \]
\end{axiom}
Betweenness is symmetric with respect to the outer points: if $B$ lies between $A$ and $C$, then it also lies between $C$ and $A$.  This reflects the fact that the segment $AC$ is the same as the segment $CA$. Left-to-right implication is used over a bicondinitional due to reduncence, as the right-to-left implication directly follows from this form of the axiom.

\begin{theorem}
  $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C) \iff \mathcal{B}(C,B,A)$ for all $A,B,C\in\mathbf{P}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  The forward direction is the axiom; the reverse follows by applying
  the axiom with $A$ and $C$ exchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{axiom}[Order]
  $\forall A,B,X\in\mathbf{P},\ \mathcal{B}(A,B,X)\ \implies\
  \bigl(\,\neg\mathcal{B}(B,A,X)\ \land\ \neg\mathcal{B}(A,X,B)\,\bigr).$
\end{axiom}
If $B$ is between $A$ and $X$, then $A$ cannot be between $B$ and $X$,
nor can $X$ be between $A$ and $B$.  In other words, a point can serve
as a ``middle'' in only one way: the triple $(A,B,X)$ determines a
unique order along the line.  Together with the Identity axiom, this
implies that $\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$ is impossible (otherwise, taking
$A=B=X$, we would have both $\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$ and its negation).
Thus the relation is \emph{non‑reflexive} in the strict sense: no
point lies between itself and itself.

\begin{axiom}[Density (inner point)]
  $\forall A,C \in \mathbf{P}, A \neq C \implies
  \exists B \in \mathbf{P} \colon \mathcal{B}(A,B,C).$
\end{axiom}
Whenever two points are distinct, there exists a point strictly between
them.  This captures the idea that segments are dense—between any two
points one can always find a third.

\begin{axiom}[Extensibility (outer point)]
  $\forall A,B\in\mathbf{P},\ A\neq B\ \implies\
  \exists C\in\mathbf{P}\colon\ \mathcal{B}(A,B,C).$
\end{axiom}
Given two distinct points, there is always a point beyond the second,
so that the second lies between the first and the new point.  This
guarantees that lines extend indefinitely.

These five axioms are mutually independent: no one can be derived from
the others (as can be shown by standard model‑theoretic arguments).
Together with the congruence axioms to be introduced later, they will
form the basis of our geometric theory.

\begin{theorem}[Irreflexivity of Betweeness]
    No point lies between it and itself:
    \[
    \forall A \in \mathbf{P},\; \neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,A).
    \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Assume $\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$. By the order axiom, this implies $\neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$ and $\neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$, which contridicts our assumption. Hence, $\neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Distinct Endpoints]
    Betweenness forces disctint outer points:
    \[
    \mathcal{B}(A,B,C) \implies A \neq C.
    \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Suppose $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$ and $A = C$. Then, we have $\mathcal{B}(A,B,A)$, which by the identity of betweenness implies $A = B$. Substituting back gives $\mathcal{B}(A,A,A)$, which contradicts the irreflexivity of betweenness. Therefore $A \neq C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Strict Betweeness]
    If $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$, then $A \neq B$ and $B \neq C$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$ and $A = B$. Then, $\mathcal{B}(A,A,C)$, which implies $\neg \mathcal{B}(A,A,C)$ by the order of betweeness. However, this is a contridiction. Thus, $A \neq B$.
Similarily, if we assume $B = C$, then we have $\mathcal{B}(A,B,B) \implies \neg \mathcal{B}(A,B,B)$, leading to a contridiction. Thus, $B \neq C$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Contrapositive Ordering]
    For all $A,B,X \in \mathbf{P}$,
    \[
    \mathcal{B}(B,A,X) \implies \neg\mathcal{B}(A,B,X)
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \mathcal{B}(A,X,B) \implies \neg\mathcal{B}(A,B,X).
    \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    The Order axiom gives the two implications
    \[
    \mathcal{B}(A,B,X) \implies \neg\mathcal{B}(B,A,X)
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \mathcal{B}(A,B,X) \implies \neg\mathcal{B}(A,X,B).
    \]
    Taking the logical contrapositive of each implication (which is 
    logically equivalent to the original) yields precisely the two 
    stated results.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Symmetry Extended]
    Given $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C)$ none of the following hold:

    $\mathcal{B}(B,A,C)$, $\mathcal{B}(A,C,B)$, $\mathcal{B}(B,C,A)$, $\mathcal{B}(C,A,B)$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Density and extensibility yield infinite points}
\label{sec:infinite}

The Density and Extensibility axioms guarantee that any pair of distinct
points can be embedded in an infinite sequence of points that behave like
a line.  We make this precise with two lemmas.

\begin{theorem}[Infinite interior points]
  \label{lem:infinite-interior}
  Let $A,C\in\mathbf{P}$ with $A\neq C$.  Then there exist infinitely many
  points strictly between $A$ and $C$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  By the Density axiom we can find a point $B_1$ such that
  $\mathcal{B}(A,B_1,C)$.  Since $A\neq C$, the Irreflexivity lemma
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:irreflexive}) and the Distinct-endpoints lemma
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:distinct-endpoints}) imply $B_1\neq A$ and $B_1\neq C$.
  Applying Density again to the pair $A,B_1$ (now distinct) yields a point
  $B_2$ with $\mathcal{B}(A,B_2,B_1)$.  Repeating this process produces an
  infinite sequence $B_1,B_2,B_3,\dots$ all distinct and satisfying
  $\mathcal{B}(A,B_{k+1},B_k)$ and consequently $\mathcal{B}(A,B_k,C)$.
  (Formally, this is an inductive construction; the existence of each new
  point is guaranteed by the Density axiom and the distinctness follows
  from the strictness of betweenness.)
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Infinite outward extension]
  \label{lem:infinite-outward}
  Let $A,B\in\mathbf{P}$ with $A\neq B$.  Then there exist infinitely many
  points $C_1,C_2,C_3,\dots$ such that
  \[
  \mathcal{B}(A,B,C_1),\quad
  \mathcal{B}(A,C_1,C_2),\quad
  \mathcal{B}(A,C_2,C_3),\quad\dots
  \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  The Extensibility axiom gives a point $C_1$ with $\mathcal{B}(A,B,C_1)$.
  Because $A\neq B$, the Distinct-endpoints lemma forces $B\neq C_1$, so in
  particular $A\neq C_1$.  Apply Extensibility to the pair $A,C_1$ to obtain
  $C_2$ with $\mathcal{B}(A,C_1,C_2)$.  Continuing in this way yields an
  infinite chain $C_1,C_2,C_3,\dots$.  The points are distinct: if
  $C_i=C_j$ for $i<j$, then repeatedly applying the Order axiom would
  contradict the irreflexivity of betweenness.
\end{proof}

Together, these two lemmas show that from any segment $AB$ ($A\neq B$) we
can generate infinitely many points in both directions along the line
determined by $A$ and $B$.  Thus the plane $\mathcal{P}$ is necessarily
infinite.

\section{Congruence}
Congruence is a quaternary relation on the set of all points denoted by $\cong \subseteq \mathbf{P}^4$. Rather than mapping individual points to one another, this relation establishes a geometric equivalence between two pairs of points. We write $\cong (A,B,C,D)$, to assert that the segment from point $A$ to point $B$ is congruent to the segment from point $C$ to point $D$. Formally:
\[
\cong (A,B,C,D) \iff (A,B,C,D) \in \cong.
\]

To reflect this conceptually, we bundle the point pairs $(A,B)$ and $(C,D)$ into the typographical units $AB$ and $CD$ and introduce the infix symbol $\cong$:
\[
AB \cong CD \overset{\text{def}}{\iff} \cong(A,B,C,D)
\]
If the relation holds for the pairs $(A, B)$ and $(C, D)$, we write $(A, B) \cong (C, D)$, which is interpreted as the "distance" from $A$ to $B$ being equal to the distance from $C$ to $D$. 

We now impose three axioms governing the behavior of $\cong$.

\begin{axiom}[Reversal of Congruence]
    \[
    \forall A, B \in \mathbf{P},\; AB \cong BA.
    \]
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Reflexivity of Congruence]
    \[
    \forall A, B \in \mathbf{P},\; AB \cong AB.
    \]
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Transitivity of Congruence]
    \[
    \forall A, B, C, D, E, F \in \mathbf{P},\; (AB \cong CD \land AB \cong EF) \implies CD \cong EF.
    \]
\end{axiom}

\begin{theorem}[Symmetry of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$, then $CD \cong AB$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We are given $AB \cong CD$. From the reflexivity of congruence theorem we also have $AB \cong AB$. Applying these two congruences to the transitivity axiom gives the antecedent
    \[
    AB \cong CD \land AB \cong AB,
    \]
    which implies $CD \cong AB$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Left-Reversal of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$, then $BA \cong CD$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We are given $AB \cong CD$. From the reversal of congruence, we also have $AB \cong BA$. Applying transitivity to the conjunction
    \[
    AB \cong BA \land AB \cong CD
    \]
    yields $BA \cong CD$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Right-Reversal of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$, then $AB \cong DC$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We are given $AB \cong CD$. From the reversal of congruence, we have $CD \cong DC$, and by the symmetry of congruence we have $CD \cong AB$. Applying transitivity to
    \[
    CD \cong AB \land CD \cong DC,
    \]
    yields $AB \cong DC$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Full-Reversal of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$, then $BA \cong DC$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We are given $AB \cong CD$. We first apply left-reversal to the expression to give $BA \cong CD$. We then apply right-reversal to obtain $BA \cong DC$.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}
    We are given $AB \cong CD$. From the reversal of congruence, we know that $AB \cong BA$. Similarily, by right-reversal we have $CD \cong DC$. Applying these to the transitivity of congruence gives
    \[
    AB \cong BA \land AB \cong DC.
    \]
    This implies $BA \cong DC$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Full-Transitivity of Congruence]
    If $AB \cong CD$ and $CD \cong EF$, then $AB \cong EF$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    We first apply symmetry to the first premise, giving $CD \cong AB$.  Then, we apply this result along with the second premise to the  transitivity axiom, giving the antecedent
    \[
    CD \cong AB \land CD \cong EF.
    \]
    This implies $AB \cong EF$, thus proving the result.
\end{proof}

With these results, we have demostrated that $\cong$ is an equivalence relation on ordered pairs of points, as it is reflexive (by axiom) and symmetric (by theorem), and fully transitive (by theorem).

Because $\cong$ is an equivalence relation, it partitions the set of all ordered point pairs into disjoint equivalence classes. Each class consists of all segments that are congruent to one another, which in geometric terms corresponds to the set of all segments having the same length. This formally captures the intuitive idea that congruence of segments is equality of distance.
